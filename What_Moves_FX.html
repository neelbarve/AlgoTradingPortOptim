What Moves FX

What the research says about news, macro releases and related markets, checked against our own hourly data for EUR/USD, GBP/USD, AUD/USD, USD/CAD and USD/JPY (July 2020 to June 2025).

Bottom line

  • News drives how much FX moves, not where it goes next. Only the surprise against consensus moves the price, within minutes. Over days, fundamentals forecast no better than a coin flip.
  • Most to least: central bank decisions and statements, US payrolls, then early US activity data (GDP advance, retail sales, durable goods, ISM, consumer confidence), inflation (sign depends on the central bank), trade balance, jobless claims, then foreign data for its own currency.
  • Direction is consistent but not fixed. Better US data lifts the dollar and worse data sinks it, and bad news moves it more. In crises the sign can flip: the dollar rose on bad US news in 2008–09.
  • In our data the payrolls hour moves each pair 3.3–5× a normal hour, and the dollar follows US Treasury yields closely in that hour (correlation 0.78). There is no follow-through in the hours after.
  • For hourly-bar strategies, news is a risk filter, not an entry signal. Use it to avoid entries and price higher costs around releases. Trade related markets instead: yields, risk appetite, oil, commodities.

2. Which way to trade: positive and negative impact

On trading the negatives "in reverse". A worse-than-expected release is not a reason to flip a strategy. 6. What is uncertain or unsolved

QuestionState of the evidence
Why fundamentals do not forecast exchange ratesOpen since Meese and Rogoff (1983): models beat a random walk only rarely out of sample.
How news reaches pricesPartly answered: about two-thirds of the effect runs through order flow (Evans & Lyons 2008).
Whether the sign is stableNo. Inflation news depends on the central bank's stance; the dollar's response flipped in 2008–09; risk conditions change the size.
Measuring the surpriseNeeds consensus forecasts, which are paid data. Yield moves are a usable proxy but mix in other news.
Unscheduled and qualitative newsFlagged as an open problem by Andersen et al.; sentiment scores now help (Filippou et al.) but methods are not standard.
Today's rankingThe best-identified rankings use 1990s–2000s data. Trade balance has likely faded; CPI likely rose after 2021 (our judgement, not a sourced finding).
Non-US newsSmaller effects and far less studied.
Trading it with hourly barsThe reaction is over within minutes; our data shows no follow-through in the next 1–6 hours.
